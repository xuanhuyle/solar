# From Research Notebook to Real Capital: How Signals Actually Earn Capital, and Where an AI Quant Researcher Fits

Between "this looks interesting" and "this may influence capital," professional firms put a signal through a human-owned chain of gates. The chain runs: investment-committee or peer review of the hypothesis and economic rationale; separate engineering review and testing of the production code; a period of out-of-sample or live-feed observation; a small initial allocation that is scaled only if live behaviour matches expectations; and continuous monitoring that ends in retirement. The evidence says that generating and backtesting ideas is getting cheap: Man Group already has AI-generated signals trading real capital. What stays scarce and human-owned is trustworthy evidence at each gate, and above all forward, live evidence. The product case is therefore strongest for the Referee, the Knowledge System and the Economic Scorecard, and weakest for the "AI researcher" itself.

## TL;DR

- **The gates are real, human-owned and fairly uniform across firms.** Before anything touches capital, firms require a hypothesis with an economic rationale, committee or peer approval, code review and testing, out-of-sample or paper evidence (Quantopian required at least 6 months), and a small, concave capital ramp. Regulators (PRA SS5/18, MiFID II RTS 6, and for energy firms REMIT II Article 5a) require documented testing, approval and decommissioning for anything that places orders algorithmically.
- **Idea generation is being commoditised fastest; forward evidence is the scarce input.** Man Group's AlphaGPT, KelAI, KX's Alpha Agent, QuantConnect's Mia and Zerve all automate hypothesis → code → backtest. Meanwhile, backtest Sharpe barely predicts live results (R² < 0.025 across 888 Quantopian algorithms), and bank alternative-beta strategies lost a median 73% of Sharpe going live. The painful gap is validated signal → live evidence → trusted scaling decision.
- **Verdict: MIXED.** Do not build a general "AI junior quant" first. The most defensible wedge is a Referee plus a tamper-evident, pre-registered forward-evidence ledger (shadow monitoring against pre-committed expectations, with decay and invalidation alerts). Target mid-size systematic teams and EU power/gas desks newly subject to REMIT II algo governance. Demand and willingness to pay are unproven and must be tested in interviews before any build.

## 1. Executive conclusion

**FACT:** The most advanced public example of an AI quant researcher, Man Group's AlphaGPT, did not change the gates. Man says AI-generated signals face "dual-track validation": the Investment Committee reviews "the underlying hypothesis, economic rationale and research outcomes using the same analytical framework applied to human-generated signals," while "technology teams conduct code reviews, implement testing protocols spanning unit tests, integration tests and scenario analysis, and assess implementation risk." Man also says "whether generated by human or AI, every strategy entering live trading must pass identical thresholds." Bloomberg reported in July 2025, citing senior portfolio manager Ziang Fang, that "several dozen signals generated by the AI system have passed Man Group's investment committee and are slated to be deployed in live trading." A 2026 Code with Claude session by Man's Tushara Fernando states that AI-researched signals are "in production at Man Group right now — running real capital," supported by a governed skills framework used by roughly 750 developers and quants with more than 100 skills.

**INFERENCE:** This is both the best validation of the thesis and the strongest argument against building it as a vendor. It validates the thesis because an AI researcher operating inside a deterministic control plane with human committee approval is demonstrably workable. It argues against a vendor because the firm that proved it built it in-house, on proprietary data and decades of institutional process, and explicitly expects the technology to commoditise ("Early-mover technology advantages are inherently temporary"). Tier-1 systematic funds are unlikely buyers of an AI researcher. They may be buyers of narrow, independent controls. Man is "expanding our monitoring infrastructure to handle increased signal volume," which suggests the bottleneck moves downstream as idea volume rises.

**INFERENCE:** The core question is what must happen between "interesting" and "capital." The answer is that three kinds of trust must be manufactured:
1. **Methodological trust:** was the test valid? This covers point-in-time data, leakage, multiple testing and economic rationale.
2. **Implementation trust:** does the production code do what the research claims? Man flags that the AI "might conceptualise one research idea but implement something different."
3. **Forward trust:** does it behave live as the backtest promised, net of costs?

Firms already have committees for the first and code review for the second. The third is the most time-consuming, the least automatable by idea-generating AI, and the most loosely documented in public practice.

## 2. Signal lifecycle map

Owners and practices below are a synthesis. Where public evidence is thin, I say so. Sophisticated firms (S) and smaller teams (T) differ materially.

| Stage | Owner | Evidence required | Code/data standards | Review | Typical failure modes | Automatable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hypothesis | Junior/senior quant; PM sets agenda | Written economic rationale; universe, horizon, costs declared | Approved datasets only | Informal (T); research-agenda review (S) | Data-mined story invented after the fact; duplicates existing book | High (Man's "Idea Person") |
| Preliminary evidence | Quant researcher | IC/returns on a restricted sample | Point-in-time data, survivorship-free | Self / peer | Look-ahead, leakage, stale data | High |
| Robust backtest | Quant researcher | Multi-regime, cost-adjusted, parameter-stable, low correlation to existing signals | Reproducible code; logged trials | Peer | Overfitting; hidden multiple testing; unrealistic costs | High (mechanics); judgement low |
| Peer / senior review | Senior quant, PM, investment committee | Research memo; rationale; trial count; correlation to the book | Documented | Committee (Man: IC; Campbell: 5-person IC plus peer review) | Persuasion over evidence; groupthink | Assist only |
| Independent validation | Model validation / risk (banks); a second researcher or tech team (funds) | Replication; implementation-equals-research check; unit, integration and scenario tests | Production-grade code | Code review; tests | Research ≠ production implementation | Partially (tests, diffing) |
| Paper / simulation | Quant + quant dev | Live-feed or embargoed out-of-sample results vs expectation | Same code path as production (Tower Research practice) | PM / risk | Too short to be statistically meaningful; cost model optimism | High (mechanics) |
| Shadow production | Quant dev / trading tech | Signal generated in production, positions computed but not traded or not weighted | Production infrastructure | PM | Divergence from research numbers; data-timing gaps | High |
| Limited capital | PM + risk | Small initial allocation; realised slippage | Risk limits; kill switch | PM / risk sign-off | Capacity and impact underestimated | Never (decision) |
| Scaling | PM / CIO / risk | Live performance within expected band | Capacity analysis | Committee | Scaling into decay | Never (decision); assist on evidence |
| Live monitoring | Quant (owner), risk, trading tech | Live vs expected IC and Sharpe, turnover, costs, exposures | Automated scripts, alerts | Periodic review | Silent decay; regime misread | High |
| Intervention | PM / risk; kill switch by trading/risk | Breach of pre-defined limits | Kill functionality (RTS 6) | Escalation procedure | Acting late; acting on noise | Alerting yes; decision no |
| Retirement | PM / committee | Sustained under-performance vs expectation; post-mortem | Decommissioning process (PRA SS5/18) | Committee | Zombie signals; no learning captured | Assist (post-mortem drafting) |

**FACT:** López de Prado's *Advances in Financial Machine Learning* describes the post-backtest part of this map as five steps: embargo (run on data after the backtest end date), paper trading ("for as long as it is needed to gather enough evidence"), graduation (a real, small position), re-allocation ("a strategy's allocation follows a concave function… initial allocation… is small… as time passes, and the strategy performs as expected, the allocation is increased"), and decommission ("when they perform below expectations for a sufficiently extended period of time"). He gives no numeric thresholds.

**ANECDOTE:** The practitioner account of Tower Research's HFT lifecycle, from ex-Flow Traders/Tower trader Annanay Kapila, describes simulation using "the exact same code path" as production and "a battery of automated scripts" watching for strategy decay after deployment.

**Smaller teams (T):** In small energy desks and mid-size funds, the researcher, developer and operator are often the same person. The Equilibrium Energy quant role spans "research, develop, backtest, and deploy," and the Engelhart power algo role spans signal evaluation, MLOps and platform development. **INFERENCE:** In these teams the "independent validation" gate is often missing or informal. That is a gap a Referee could fill, but it also means there may be no one whose job is to buy it.

## 3. Promotion gates

**INFERENCE (from Man, López de Prado, PRA and Quantopian evidence):** There are five gates that actually matter, in this order:

1. **Research gate (hypothesis → accepted research).** The decision is whether the effect is real, rationalised and non-redundant. Owner: senior quant / committee.
2. **Committee gate (accepted research → approved for production).** The decision is whether we believe it enough to spend engineering and risk budget. Owner: investment committee / PM. At Campbell & Company, "new models and parameters are approved by humans: by Campbell's five-person investment committee and a peer review process," and "a concept might take 6 months to go from an idea to the investment committee though incubation periods do vary" (Hedge Fund Journal).
3. **Implementation gate (approved → deployable).** The decision is whether the code matches the research and is safe. Owner: technology / quant dev. For firms in scope of algo rules this is a regulatory gate. PRA SS5/18 expects "all algorithms (including those provided by external vendors) and risk controls to be tested prior to deployment" and states "any variation of an algorithm (for example, regional variation) should be classified as a new algorithm and therefore subject to separate testing and approval."
4. **Capital gate (deployable → funded).** The decision is how much and when. Owner: PM / CIO / risk. The typical practice is a small initial allocation scaled on live evidence. **ANECDOTE / UNVERIFIED:** the Delphic Alpha Substack post "Signals Die. What Happens Next Is What Matters." paraphrases Campbell's Kevin Cole as saying Campbell runs "approximately 130 individual models," adds "5 to 10 new models per year and remove[s] 1 to 2," and puts each new model through "a phased deployment starting at one-tenth of target capital." I could not verify this against a transcript.
5. **Survival gate (funded → retained or retired).** The decision is whether it is still behaving as expected. Owner: PM / committee, with risk-driven hard limits.

**FACT:** Regulation formalises gates 3–5 for algorithmic order placement. PRA SS5/18 requires firms to "define the term 'algorithm'," "prescribe the process for the approval and decommissioning of an algorithm," and "outline the testing and validation process… including who has responsibility," with periodic re-validation "commensurate with the risks." **UNKNOWN:** whether an alpha signal that feeds a portfolio optimiser, rather than directly setting order parameters, is itself an "algorithm" under these regimes. Firms define this themselves, and the answer determines whether a signal promotion is a regulated event.

## 4. Evidence required at each gate

| Gate | Evidence that practitioners actually weigh | What an honest package must also disclose |
|---|---|---|
| Research | Economic rationale; out-of-sample IC/Sharpe; stability across regimes/sub-universes; turnover; cost sensitivity | Number of trials run (for deflated Sharpe / multiple-testing adjustment); data vintage |
| Committee | Research memo; correlation to existing book (WorldQuant BRAIN, for example, gates on self-correlation < 0.7); capacity estimate; expected decay | Failed variants; what would falsify it |
| Implementation | Code review; unit/integration/scenario tests; research-vs-production reconciliation | Any deviation from the researched specification |
| Capital | Paper/shadow results vs pre-stated expectation; realised vs modelled costs | Length of forward sample relative to the statistical power needed |
| Survival | Live vs expected distribution; attribution; regime diagnostics | Post-mortem for retirements |

**FACT (why forward evidence dominates):**
- Wiecki et al. (Quantopian, 888 algorithms with at least 6 months out of sample) found that backtest Sharpe offers "little value in predicting out of sample performance (R² < 0.025)." They also found that "the more backtesting a quant has done for a strategy, the larger the discrepancy between backtest and out-of-sample performance."
- Suhonen, Lennkh & Perez found, across 215 bank alternative-beta strategies, a "median 73% deterioration in Sharpe ratios between backtested and live" (median 1.20 → 0.31).
- McLean & Pontiff found anomaly returns 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower post-publication.
- Harvey & Liu report that "a common practice in evaluating backtests of trading strategies is to discount the reported Sharpe ratios by 50%." They argue the right haircut is non-linear: at most about 25% for Sharpe above 1.0, and far more than 50% for weak strategies.

**ANECDOTE:** Ex-Man AHL PM Rob Carver: "I do not trust a back tested Sharpe ratio over two."

**INFERENCE:** At every gate, the most valuable evidence is (a) an honest count of what was tried and (b) forward results measured against expectations committed in advance. Both are exactly what an AI generating hundreds of hypotheses makes harder to trust, and what a tamper-evident ledger makes easier.

## 5. Paper / shadow trading practices

**Definitions as used in practice (INFERENCE, synthesised from López de Prado, Quantopian, NautilusTrader and Tower accounts):**
- **Simulation / embargo test:** running the frozen strategy over data after the backtest end date, without live infrastructure.
- **Paper trading:** running on a live real-time feed with simulated fills. Quantopian: "There's no way to 'overfit' an algorithm that is in paper trading mode - this is an out-of-sample test that simply can't be beaten."
- **Shadow production:** the signal is computed inside the production stack, and positions or target weights are generated and logged but not traded, or traded at zero weight. It tests data timing, infrastructure and implementation fidelity as well as alpha.
- **Canary / limited capital:** real orders at small size to measure real slippage and impact. This is the only stage that validates cost assumptions.

**How long?**
- **FACT:** Quantopian staff: "We generally wait for strategies to accumulate 6 months of out-of-sample data before evaluating." The authors of the 888-algorithm study "adopted a requirement of least 6 months of OOS data."
- **FACT:** López de Prado gives no fixed duration ("as long as it is needed").
- **ANECDOTE:** Man AHL has strategies that "have been incubated internally for years before being opened to any external allocations" (fund-level, not signal-level).
- **UNKNOWN:** signal-level incubation durations at named institutional funds, prop firms or power desks. No primary source gives "X months at small size, then scale."
- **INFERENCE:** Duration is set by statistical power relative to signal frequency. An intraday power or HFT signal can accumulate thousands of observations in weeks; a monthly equity factor cannot in a year. This is why shadow periods for slow signals are more about implementation fidelity than about proving alpha.

**Metrics that matter:** live vs expected IC and hit rate; Sharpe relative to the backtest confidence band (Quantopian used a "Bayesian cone projection" to judge whether a losing algorithm was "unlikely to return" to its starting capital); turnover vs modelled; realised vs modelled costs; exposure drift; correlation to the existing book.

**Transaction costs:**
- **FACT (ANECDOTE-grade, but primary and numerical):** Carver's second live year of futures trading showed a Sharpe "just over 1.0; better than expected in the back test at around 0.88." Total trading costs were 1.39% vs a backtested 1.5%, but the components diverged in opposite directions: slippage was 0.63% vs 1.0% modelled, and commissions were 0.76% vs 0.5% modelled.
- **INFERENCE:** Aggregate cost reconciliation can hide offsetting component errors. A monitor must reconcile component by component.
- **FACT:** In power markets, vendors quote execution improvements of "0.15 to 2.00 €/MWh on position closing" (Volue AlgoTrader, a vendor claim). This shows how much of energy-desk P&L sits in execution rather than prediction.

## 6. Live monitoring and retirement

**FACT:** Monitoring is standard at sophisticated firms. Tower is described as having automated KPI scripts watching for decay from "changes in market microstructure, new competitors entering the same signal space, exchange-level modifications, or even regressions in the firm's own code." Man is expanding monitoring infrastructure for AI-generated signal volume.

**FACT (vendor):** KX markets "continuous live-vs-backtest drift analysis" in which each model's live output is benchmarked against its historical behaviour and expected distribution. It ran a February 2026 webinar titled "Signal Decay: Why Alpha Half-Lives Are Shrinking and How Leading Funds Keep Up." KX's quoted ROI figures (for example "$16.3M in annual alpha uplift") are unaudited marketing claims.

**Retirement rules:**
- **ANECDOTE:** On the Top Traders Unplugged podcast (as quoted by the Delphic Alpha Substack), Toby Crabel says Crabel Capital Management shifts roughly 10% of its portfolio every year: "If we didn't do that… our five or ten-year-old portfolio would have put us out of business, probably."
- **ANECDOTE / secondary:** The Wall Street Journal, as relayed by The WealthAdvisor, reported that a Millennium manager "overseeing $1 billion will see their capital halved if they lose 5% of their allocation" and that "if losses reach 7.5%… the manager is typically fired, although exceptions are occasionally made." These are PM-level, not signal-level, limits.
- **UNKNOWN:** no primary source for signal-level "retire if live Sharpe < Z for N months" rules at a named firm.
- **INFERENCE:** Signal retirement is judgement-driven and usually triggered by a pattern (decay relative to expectation plus a plausible cause) rather than a single threshold. The weak point is distinguishing crowding-driven permanent decay from temporary regime drawdown. That is precisely where structured research memory (under which regimes has this signal previously drawn down and recovered?) adds value.

**Post-mortems:** **UNKNOWN** how systematically firms record them. **INFERENCE:** Recording them is the natural job of the Research Knowledge System, and a likely weak spot at mid-size teams.

**Could continuous signal monitoring be a first-class product feature?** **INFERENCE: Yes, and it is the most defensible feature,** for three reasons:
1. Its output (live vs pre-committed expectation) is objective, which neutralises the "persuasive prose" problem.
2. It compounds: each signal's live record becomes proprietary memory.
3. It is the one place where an external, independent tool has a credibility advantage over the researcher, human or AI, who built the signal.

The counter-risk is that data-infrastructure vendors (KX), research platforms (KelAI claims "live monitoring"; QuantConnect advertises agentic "live monitoring") and in-house scripts already occupy parts of it.

| Role | Primary responsibilities in the lifecycle |
|---|---|
| Junior quant | Data prep, backtest execution, implementing variants, first-pass monitoring |
| Senior quant | Research agenda, methodology, peer review, signal ownership, decay diagnosis |

| Risk | Limits, kill switches, escalation, sign-off on controls tests (PRA SS5/18 expects all relevant functions to sign off control tests) |
| Model validation | Independent review (formal in banks under SR 11-7 / PRA SS1/23; informal or absent in many funds and energy desks) |
| Compliance | Algo inventory, regulatory notifications (REMIT II Article 5a: notify NRA and ACER; keep records 5 years), surveillance |
| Data engineering | Point-in-time datasets, vendor onboarding, data quality |
| Trading technology | Execution, connectivity, latency/capacity testing, kill functionality |

**INFERENCE:** Separating the proposer from the validator is standard in banks, partial in large funds (committee plus technology review) and weakest in small teams. The product's Researcher/Referee separation mirrors real organisational design, which is a strength of the thesis.

## 9. Where human authority must remain

- **Methodology approval:** which tests, holdouts and thresholds count. The Referee enforces rules, but humans set them.
- **Committee approval** of any signal for production (Man keeps this even for its own AI).
- **Production code acceptance:** under PRA SS5/18, vendor-provided algorithms must also be tested and approved by the firm.
- **Capital allocation, sizing, scaling and retirement:** P&L owners and risk.
- **Kill-switch operation and limit breaches:** risk and trading technology, as regulators require.
- **Regulatory attestations** (algo inventories, REMIT II notifications).

**INFERENCE:** An AI that can modify its own evaluation criteria, holdouts or the forward ledger would destroy the product's value. Tamper-evidence is a core requirement, not a feature.

## 10. Best product insertion point

**Assessment of the four candidate transitions:**

| Transition | Pain | Competition | Fit for a solo non-quant founder | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Notebook → robust experiment | Medium; well served by in-house frameworks | Crowded: QuantConnect Mia, Zerve, KX Alpha Agent, KelAI, WorldQuant-style tooling, Claude Code in-house | Low (credibility gap) | Avoid as lead |
| Experiment → review package | Medium-high; volume of AI-generated candidates overwhelms committees | Thin | Medium | Strong secondary |
| Validated signal → live shadow monitor | High; the most time-consuming and least standardised step; decides capital | Partial (KX, in-house scripts, KelAI claims) | Medium | **Lead wedge** |
| Active signal → decay / invalidation monitor | High but episodic | Partial (KX drift, in-house) | Medium | Bundle with shadow monitor |

**Recommendation (INFERENCE):** Lead with a **"forward-evidence ledger"**. The product would:
- pre-register each promoted signal's expected live distribution (IC, Sharpe band, turnover, cost components, correlation to the book) at the committee gate;
- run it in shadow on the firm's own infrastructure;
- produce tamper-evident live-vs-expected reports and decay/invalidation alerts;
- write every outcome into structured research memory.

Package the "experiment → review package" step on the front end: an honest trial count, data vintage and a falsification statement. The AI researcher then becomes an optional upstream module rather than the core promise.

**Target segments, compared:**
- **Tier-1 systematic funds:** low likelihood. They build in-house (Man), and "funds trust AI with their workflow much more than they trust it with their alpha" (Daryl Smith, head of research at Neudata, quoted by Hedgeweek in September 2026).
- **Mid-size systematic funds and multi-manager systematic pods:** plausible. Rising signal volume from their own AI tooling, thinner validation staff, and allocator due diligence increasingly auditing AI processes.
- **EU power/gas trading desks:** plausible, for a different reason. REMIT II Article 5a (modelled on MiFID II Article 17) requires notification of algorithmic trading to national regulators and ACER, records kept for five years, and descriptions covering strategies, parameters, testing methodology and risk controls. Algos reportedly execute "50%+ of intraday power volume on some EPEX markets" (Brady, vendor claim). But energy-desk algos are largely execution and asset-optimisation logic around forecasts, not alpha signals. **INFERENCE:** the product would need to monitor forecast-driven strategies and execution quality, not just equity-style IC.

## Caveats

- Private firm workflows are only partly observable. Most lifecycle detail comes from a handful of firms that publish (Man, Campbell, Quantopian), from books, and from vendor or Substack material of mixed quality. Signal-level incubation durations and retirement thresholds remain **UNKNOWN**.
- Several specific numbers are secondary or unverified: Campbell's "one-tenth of target capital" ramp (a Delphic Alpha paraphrase) and Millennium's 5%/7.5% limits (WSJ via The WealthAdvisor).
- Vendor ROI claims (KX) and startup traction claims (KelAI's signals "running since October 2025") are self-reported.
- Regulatory applicability is unsettled. Whether a signal feeding an optimiser is an "algorithm," and how MiFID II's ancillary-activity exemption interacts with REMIT II for energy firms, varies by firm and jurisdiction. Reed Smith notes that MiFID II/RTS 6 applies to power and gas futures but not to short-term physical trading, although many physical participants align with it voluntarily.

## Implications for the AI Quant Researcher Thesis

### 1. What the evidence supports
- AI-generated signals can clear institutional gates and trade real capital when embedded in a governed process (Man AlphaGPT; roughly 750 users and 100+ governed skills).
- The Researcher/Referee separation mirrors real organisational practice: committee review plus independent technology review, and proposer/validator separation in banks and regulation.
- Multiple testing, look-ahead and implementation drift are the named risks of AI research. Man lists "hallucination, lookahead bias, multiple testing" and implementing something different from the stated idea. These are exactly what a deterministic control plane addresses.
- Forward evidence dominates backtest evidence (R² < 0.025; median 73% Sharpe deterioration; 50% haircut folklore). This supports the Economic Scorecard and forward ledger as the core.
- Regulatory tailwinds require documented testing, approval, inventory and decommissioning (PRA SS5/18, RTS 6), and newly extend to EU energy algo traders (REMIT II Article 5a).

### 2. What the evidence weakens
- **The "AI junior quant" as the lead product.** Idea → code → backtest is the most crowded and fastest-commoditising layer (Man, KelAI, KX, QuantConnect Mia V2, Zerve, Resiliq, plus in-house Claude Code). Man itself expects commoditisation.
- **Sale to tier-1 funds.** They build in-house and guard signal IP. The Lowenstein Sandler survey (107 respondents, Nov–Dec 2025) shows data ownership/privacy (42%), cybersecurity (41%) and vendor-vetting burden (40%) as top AI-purchase concerns.
- **The founder–market fit of a solo non-quant.** KelAI's founder ran a large systematic book at WorldQuant. Buyers of validation tools will test the vendor's methodological credibility first.
- **A clean energy-desk fit.** Their algos are mostly execution and asset optimisation, their teams are small and generalist, and algo compliance may be bought from execution vendors (Volue, Brady) rather than from a research-validation tool.

### 3. What remains unknown and cannot be resolved through desk research
- Actual signal-level incubation durations, ramp schedules and retirement thresholds at named firms.
- Whether mid-size funds would pay for independent forward-evidence tooling, or view it as a few hundred lines of in-house code.
- Whether committees want pre-registration (it constrains PM discretion) or would resist it.
- Whether firms treat signal promotion as a regulated "algorithm" change.
- Whether energy-desk compliance teams see REMIT II testing documentation as a pain they would buy software to solve, and who holds the budget.
- Whether a vendor can run shadow monitoring without touching proprietary positions and data (on-prem or air-gapped requirements).

### 4. The 5 most important questions to ask practitioners
1. "Walk me through the last signal you promoted: who approved each step, how long did it sit in paper or shadow before any capital, and what exact evidence moved it to the next size?"
2. "When a live signal under-performs, how do you decide whether it is decayed or in a normal drawdown, and what did you record the last time you retired one?"
3. "How do you know how many variants were tried before a backtest reached committee, and would you trust that number if an AI generated the research?"
4. "Where do live-vs-backtest reconciliations live today (scripts, spreadsheets, a vendor), who maintains them, and what broke last time?"
5. (Energy desks) "For REMIT II Article 5a, what do you have to document about algorithm testing and changes, who produces it, and what would you pay to make it automatic and audit-ready?"

### 5. Verdict: **MIXED**
The evidence supports the architecture: separating the Researcher from the Referee, with a Knowledge System and an Economic Scorecard. It does not support leading with the AI researcher. Build first, and only after roughly 20 interviews confirm budget and pain, a tamper-evident Referee plus forward-evidence ledger ("validated signal → shadow → decay monitor"), with an evidence-package generator in front. Target mid-size systematic teams and EU power/gas desks under REMIT II. Kill the project if interviews show that (a) firms will not let an external tool see signal outputs even on-prem, (b) live-vs-expected monitoring is considered a solved in-house script, or (c) no one below CIO level holds budget for independent validation.
